[0]   Verse 6: Health (Arogya) is the supreme foundation for achieving the four universal pursuits of life: Dharma (righteousness), Artha (wealth), Kama (fulfillment of desires), and Moksha (spiritual liberation). Diseases are the thieves that plunder this health, life itself, and human well-being.

[1]   Verses 7–8: Diseases are classified into four broad categories:

[2] Sharira (Somatic): Diseases born of the internal vitiation of bodily humors—Vata, Pitta, and Kapha—such as fevers and skin disorders.

[3] Manasa (Psychic): Mental afflictions arising from passions such as anger, greed, fear, and sorrow.

[4] Agantu (Extrinsic/Traumatic): Afflictions resulting from curses, microbial/toxic factors, or physical injuries.

[5] Sahaja (Natural/Congenital): Natural conditions experienced since birth, including hunger, thirst, sleep, and aging.

[6]   Verse 9: The natural balance and equilibrium of the bodily humors (Dhatus and Doshas) is termed health (Arogya or Sukha). Any disequilibrium, deficiency, or excess is termed disease, known under the synonyms Vyadhi, Roga, Amaya, Gada, and Vikara.

[7] Prognosis & Etiological Origins

[8] Verse 10: Broadly, diseases are twofold: curable (Sadhya) and incurable (Asadhya).

[9] Curable diseases are divided into Sukha-sadhya (easily curable with minimal discomfort or intervention) and Kricchra-sadhya (curable with difficulty and intensive effort).

[10] Incurable diseases are divided into Yapya (manageable/palliative, persisting throughout life with continuous treatment) and Anupakrama or Pratyakhyeya (untreatable/fatal; a physician should refrain from intervening).

[11] Verse 11: From another perspective, diseases are twofold: those born of sinful acts (Papaja) and those born of past actions (Karmaja). Those arising from sins subside through expiation, charity, and medicinal therapy.

[12] Verse 12: When a disease is treated strictly in accordance with scriptural guidelines, yet does not yield to suitable medications, it must be recognized as born of unalterable karma (Karmaja).

[13] Verse 13: No living being born on this earth is immortal. Therefore, the physician cannot prevent death when its destined time arrives, but curable diseases can and must be treated and alleviated.

[14] Progression of Neglected Diseases and Causes of Death

[15] Verse 14: If a curable disease is neglected through improper care or lack of intervention, it degenerates into a palliative (Yapya) condition. If a palliative disease is neglected, it inevitably turns incurable (Asadhya), swiftly destroying the life of the person.

[16] Verse 15: Some diseases are palliative by their very inherent nature, while others become palliative through neglect. Likewise, some diseases are incurable by nature, whereas others turn incurable due to lack of timely treatment.

[17] Verses 16–17: In this human body, one hundred and one forms of death reside constantly. Among these, one is designated as Kala (timely death brought about by destiny/old age), for which no medical remedy exists. The remaining one hundred are designated as Agantu (accidental or untimely deaths). Untimely afflictions can be successfully pacified through medicinal treatment, incantations, sacrificial rites, and charitable deeds, but the destined time of death (Kala) cannot be averted by any means.

[18] Limits of Medicine and Untimely Death

[19] Verses 18–20: When the final destined hour arrives, even Lord Dhanvantari himself cannot prolong life or restore health to the embodied soul. Astrological and philosophical treatises affirm that when past merit (Karma) and lifespan (Ayu) are exhausted, neither medications, mantras, sacrificial fires, nor spiritual chants can grant protection to man.

[20] Verses 21–22: Lifespan is likened to a vessel of oil sustained by a lamp wick. Just as a gust of wind can extinguish a lamp even when oil and wick remain, sudden diseases or injuries cause untimely death (Apakala Mrityu) even when residual lifespan exists. The physician's expertise lies solely in identifying signs of life, managing imbalances, and removing avoidable obstacles to recovery; the physician is neither the ultimate arbiter nor the master of a human being's lifespan.

[21] Patients to Reject & The Urgency of Immediate Intervention

[22] Verses 23–24: A physician must never treat a patient who is undisciplined, who eats and acts arbitrarily without self-restraint, who is destitute of attendants and necessary resources, who harbors malice toward the physician or scripture, or who is suspicious, disobedient, or devoid of faith. A physician who undertakes the treatment of such individuals incurs grave professional and moral faults.

[23] Verse 25: As long as vital breath remains within the throat and sensory faculties are not completely extinguished, therapy should be continued with vigilance, for the course of time and fate is intricate and unpredictable.

[24] Verse 26: A disease must never be ignored at its very inception under the assumption that it is trivial. Even a minute spark of fire, a drop of poison, or a minor foreign body can grow to destroy a man; in the same way, an initially mild disease, if neglected, rapidly assumes fatal proportions.

[25] Stages of Disease, Astrological Alignments, and Modes of Therapy

[26] Verse 27: Just as a tender young sapling can be easily uprooted with minimal effort, but requires monumental exertion once it grows into a massive tree, so too a disease can be conquered easily at its onset, but becomes exceedingly difficult to eradicate once deeply rooted in the tissues.

[27] Verse 28: If the planetary influences and destiny of the patient are adverse, even the most potent medicine fails to take effect, for adverse fate destroys the therapeutic potency of remedies.

[28] Verse 29: The wise physician must first examine prognostic indicators, planetary configurations, and the physical constitution before initiating therapy. Therapeutic action (Chikitsa) is classified into three types:

[29] Asuri (Demonic/Surgical): Treatment involving surgical incision, alkali application, cauterization, and bloodletting.

[30] Manushi (Human/Herbal): Treatment relying on decoctions, dietary regimens, churnas, and herbal formulations.

[31] Daivi (Divine/Rasa Shastra): Treatment utilizing processed minerals, incinerated metals (Bhasmas), gems, and mercurial preparations.

[32] Verse 30: That intervention which brings equilibrium to the bodily tissues (Dhatus) without provoking another ailment is genuine medical treatment (Chikitsa).

[33] The Physician's Practice, Cooking Rules, and The Medical Quadruple

[34] Verse 31: Proper treatment consists of balancing deranged humors, and this constitutes the sacred duty of the physician. Therefore, the physician must execute clinical duties diligently and without hesitation or fear.

[35] Verse 32: In the practice of medicine, a physician gains religious merit (Dharma) in some cases, material wealth (Artha) in others, renown (Yashas) elsewhere, and constant practical experience (Karmabhyasa) in all cases. Medical practice is never futile.

[36] Verses 33–34: The preparation of dietary supplements and medicinal confections (Paka) should be assigned to individuals of suitable cleanliness and caste integrity. If food or medicinal preparations are mishandled or contaminated during processing, it causes impurity and requires religious atonement.

[37] Verse 35 (Transition): Successful eradication of disease relies upon the four limbs of therapy (Chikitsa Chatushpada): the Physician, the Medicament, the Attendant, and the Patient. When all four are endowed with their necessary qualities, they bring about the swift pacification of disorders.
